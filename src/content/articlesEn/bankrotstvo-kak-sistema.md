## 1. The Myth of Private Failure

When a company enters bankruptcy proceedings, public discourse typically frames it as a story of mismanagement, bad luck, or market forces. The owner «lost everything»; the market «punished inefficiency». This narrative is comforting because it individualizes failure. But it obscures a fundamental truth: corporate bankruptcy is a **systemic redistribution mechanism**.

The debts of a failed enterprise do not disappear. They are restructured, written off, or socialized. The question is not *whether* losses will be distributed, but *to whom* and *through what procedures*. And in most jurisdictions, the procedure is designed to protect certain creditors while leaving others — employees, small suppliers, local communities  at the bottom of the hierarchy.

## 2. The Hierarchy of Loss

Bankruptcy law creates a formal order of priority. Secured creditors — typically banks holding collateral — are paid first. Then come unsecured creditors: bondholders, suppliers, contractors. Employees' wage claims and social insurance contributions follow. Shareholders, the nominal «owners» of the enterprise, are last.

This hierarchy appears rational. It protects the financial system by ensuring that lenders retain confidence. But it also means that **the institutional actors who facilitated the risky behavior are the first to be protected from its consequences**. Banks that issued loans against overvalued assets, auditors who signed off on optimistic valuations, and regulators who failed to intervene early — all retain their positions while the costs cascade downward.

Consider the case of a mid-sized manufacturing plant in a single-industry town. When the holding company defaults, the bank seizes the real estate. The employees, who have not been paid for three months, become creditors in a process they do not control. The local budget loses tax revenue. The supply chain collapses, dragging down smaller firms that had no direct relationship with the holding company's management.

## 3. Intentional Bankruptcy: A Tool, Not a Tragedy

Not all bankruptcies are unintended. In environments with weak creditor protection and opaque ownership structures, bankruptcy can be deployed as a **deliberate instrument**.

The scheme is depressingly consistent. Assets are transferred to affiliated entities at nominal prices. Debts are concentrated in the original company. When bankruptcy is declared, the valuable assets are already beyond the reach of creditors. The procedure becomes a formalized mechanism for **expropriation dressed in legal clothing**.

Russian arbitrazh practice has documented hundreds of such cases. The tools for combating them — subsidiary liability, challenging preferential transactions, piercing the corporate veil — exist in the statute books. But their application requires resources, expertise, and time that unsecured creditors rarely possess.

## 4. The Socialization of Private Risk

The ultimate recipient of bankruptcy costs is often the state — and, by extension, the taxpayer. Unpaid wages become the responsibility of regional welfare funds. Pension obligations shift to state guarantees. Environmental damage left by abandoned industrial sites requires public cleanup budgets.

This is the **socialization of private risk**. The profits of enterprise activity were privatized; the losses are socialized. The mechanism is not always visible because it operates through budget lines, regional subsidies, and delayed infrastructure projects rather than direct payments.

In the 2022–2024 period, several high-profile corporate collapses in Russia's construction and retail sectors demonstrated this pattern. Shareholders withdrew dividends in the years preceding insolvency. Management bonuses were paid while wage arrears accumulated. The formal bankruptcy procedure lasted years, during which asset values depreciated and creditors' recoveries dwindled.

## 5. What the Documents Show

Court files reveal patterns that press releases do not. In case after case, the same signatures appear: the same auditors certifying financial statements months before collapse; the same banks restructuring loans repeatedly; the same lawyers representing both debtors and creditors in different proceedings.

The documents also reveal the **time lag between distress and action**. In most cases, insolvency was foreseeable 12–24 months before the formal filing. By the time the petition reaches the court, the meaningful assets are often already encumbered or transferred.

## 6. Conclusion: Bankruptcy as a Mirror

Corporate bankruptcy does not merely resolve a financial failure. It reveals the **power structure** of an economic system. Who is protected first? Who waits longest? Who has no standing at all?

The answer to these questions tells us more about the nature of an economy than any GDP figure. A system where employees and small suppliers bear the costs of managerial failure is not a market economy in any meaningful sense. It is a system of **privatized gains and socialized losses** — maintained by legal procedures that formalize and legitimize the transfer.

Understanding bankruptcy as a system, rather than a collection of individual misfortunes, is the first step toward asking the question that ACTA FRAGILIA considers central: **who pays for the decisions made by those who do not pay themselves?**
